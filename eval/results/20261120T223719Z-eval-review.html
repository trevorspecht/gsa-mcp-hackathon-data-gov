Four useful datasets cover drought conditions, runoff vulnerability, and irrigated land in the western United States:

Dataset Coverage and period What it provides
Western Land Data Assimilation System (WLDAS), NASA Western U.S. and surrounding region; catalog period January 2, 1979–January 1, 2024; daily, 0.01° grid Modeled soil moisture, snow depth and snow-water equivalent, evapotranspiration, groundwater recharge, and anomalies. A useful starting point for historical drought and water-availability analysis—not direct field measurements.
Runoff vulnerability to the 2015 western U.S. drought, USGS 326 western river basins, focused on 2015 Measures assessing streamflow vulnerability to drought. Appropriate for a basin-level drought case study rather than ongoing monitoring.
LANID: Landsat-based irrigation dataset for CONUS 2018–20, USGS Conterminous U.S., including the West; annual irrigation maps for 2018–2020 Identifies irrigated crops. Metadata caveat: the description also discusses a monthly 2000–2020 HUC12 irrigation-water-use reanalysis, including evapotranspiration, consumptive use, and effective precipitation. Confirm which files belong to this record before assuming those longer series are included.
Landsat-derived Rainfed and Irrigated-Cropland Product, L3 V002 Currently conterminous U.S., despite “Global” in the title; nominal 2020, using 2019–2021 imagery; 30 m Maps irrigated cropland, rainfed cropland, non-cropland, and water bodies. Useful for locating irrigation, but not measuring irrigation-water volumes.

Question: Is there a correlation between climate patterns and agricultural practices over time in Maryland and the Chesapeake Bay watershed?

Expected: Answer uncertainty first; mismatched geography and sparse practice years prevent defensible correlation/causation. Useful verified descriptive context, source evidence, relevant alternative discovery/readiness.

Agent answer

The verified data show changes in climate conditions and agricultural practices, but they do not establish a correlation between them. That is a limitation of the available evidence—not evidence that no relationship exists.

What the historical observations show

Measure Geography and period Verified observations

Sources: NOAA NCEI’s Maryland temperature and precipitation series, catalog C00005; USGS/Chesapeake Bay Program agricultural estimates, DOI 10.5066/P9SRZS8P.

Why this does not demonstrate correlation

  • Geographies differ: Maryland statewide climate does not represent the entire Chesapeake watershed.
  • Practice observations are sparse: cover crops have only two observation years; conservation tillage has four.
  • Endpoint changes are not trend estimates. Annual weather fluctuations also should not be mistaken for a long-term climate trend.
  • Agricultural areas are spatially allocated estimates of reported practices, not adoption rates or direct measurements everywhere. Practices can overlap, so their areas should not be added.
  • The practices were reported for soil and water-quality management; these records do not establish climate-driven adoption.

Consequently, reporting a correlation coefficient from these series would be misleading. These are historical snapshots ending in 2012, not current conditions.

More directly relevant evidence

A targeted catalog search also found USGS studies combining climate, stream conditions and management gradients: 30 agricultural-region streams in Pennsylvania/Maryland during March–September 2023 (DOI 10.5066/P13EMSZU), plus a 2024 Developed Piedmont study (DOI 10.5066/P14XBLPB). These could inform local relationships, but are not long-term adoption studies. Their catalog routes advertise XML metadata; underlying file schemas, dictionaries and detailed use terms remain unverified, and they are not currently queryable here.

Bottom line: testing the relationship requires repeated climate and practice observations for matching locations and years. Establishing why practices changed would additionally require evidence separating climate influences from policy, incentives and other management drivers.
